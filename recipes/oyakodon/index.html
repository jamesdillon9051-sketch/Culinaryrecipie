Oyakodon

Chicken and egg over rice — the name means "parent and child" — simmered together in a sweet-savoury dashi until the egg is barely set and slides over the bowl in soft curds. A fifteen-minute dinner with the comfort of a two-hour one.

25 min total Serves 2 servings Easy

Don't Make These Mistakes

Make one portion at a time in a small pan. Doubling it in a big pan gives you scrambled chicken, not oyakodon.

Instant dashi powder is entirely acceptable here and is what most Japanese home cooks use on a weeknight.

A donburi pan with a lid is traditional, but any small lidded frying pan does the same job.

Making Oyakodon

Tap a step to highlight it, or turn on Cook Mode for large type and step-by-step focus.

  1. Beat the eggs with chopsticks just enough to streak the white through the yolk. Over-beating gives a uniform, omelette-like texture instead of soft ribbons.

  2. Combine the dashi, soy, mirin, sake and sugar in a small frying pan, about 20 cm, and bring to a simmer.

  3. Add the onion and simmer 3 minutes until translucent.

  4. Lay the chicken in a single layer and simmer 5 minutes, turning once, until just cooked through.

  5. Pour two-thirds of the egg over the surface in a spiral. Cover and cook 45 seconds without stirring.

  6. Pour over the remaining egg, cover, and cook 30 seconds more. The top should still look wet.

  7. Slide the whole thing over a bowl of hot rice, scatter with spring onion and shichimi, and eat immediately.

What's Going On Here

The egg is added in two stages and taken off the heat while still visibly loose, because residual heat from the pan and the rice finishes it — a fully set oyakodon is a rubbery one. Cooking it in a small pan means the liquid stays deep enough to poach the egg rather than fry it.

Leftovers, Reheating and Keeping

Eat straight away — the egg firms and the rice goes soggy on standing. The sauce base keeps 5 days refrigerated.

Swaps and Variations

  • Out of spring onions? A small regular onion, finely sliced, or a few chives stirred in at the end both cover the job.

Dietary Notes

  • For a gluten-free version, use a tamari or soy sauce labelled gluten-free in place of the soy sauce: ordinary soy sauce is brewed with wheat, and not every tamari is wheat-free.
