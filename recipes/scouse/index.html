Scouse

Liverpool's stew of beef, onions, carrots and potatoes, some of which break down to thicken it. Served with pickled red cabbage and crusty bread.

Why This Is Worth Making

Scouse is the stew of Liverpool, so closely tied to the city that its people are known as Scousers. It is a relative of lobscouse, the salted-meat stew eaten by sailors across northern Europe, and it probably came into the port on the ships, where it was a cheap, filling dish for people who had little to spend. Its main features have not changed. It is made from beef or lamb, with onions, carrots and a large quantity of potatoes, cooked slowly in stock. The important detail is that some of the potatoes are cut small and cooked until they collapse, thickening the stew into a rich, starchy gravy, and the rest are left in chunks, so that there is something to bite. It has a meatless version, known as blind scouse, which was made when there was no meat to be had. Scouse is traditionally served with pickled red cabbage or beetroot, whose sharpness cuts through the richness, and with crusty bread to mop up the gravy. The stew improves on the second day, and it is a good dish for a cold evening.

What Goes With It

  • Pickled red cabbage
  • Crusty bread
  • Buttered bread and beetroot

How to Cook Scouse

Tap a step to highlight it, or turn on Cook Mode for large type and step-by-step focus.

  1. Season the beef with the salt and pepper.

  2. Heat the dripping in a large, heavy pot over medium-high heat and brown the beef in two batches for 4 minutes each, until deep golden. Lift it onto a plate.

  3. Add the onions to the pot and cook for 6 minutes, scraping up the browned bits, until soft.

  4. Return the beef to the pot, add the stock, Worcestershire sauce, bay leaves and thyme and bring to the boil. Cover, reduce the heat, and simmer gently for 1 hour 15 minutes, until the beef is almost tender.

  5. Stir in the carrots and the small diced potatoes and simmer, covered, for 30 minutes, until the small potatoes have broken down and thickened the stew.

  6. Add the potato chunks and simmer for 25 minutes more, until they are tender but still hold their shape.

  7. Remove the bay leaves, taste for salt and pepper, and stir in the parsley.

  8. Serve in deep bowls with pickled red cabbage and crusty bread.

Worth Knowing Before You Cook

Cut the potatoes in two sizes. The small pieces melt and thicken the stew, and the large ones stay as chunks.

Stir gently after the potatoes go in. They break up easily, and a stew that has to be stirred hard is too thick.

Serve it with pickled red cabbage, which is not an optional extra. The sharp vinegar is the traditional counterpoint to the rich, starchy stew.

Common Substitutions & Variations

  • Fresh herbs work in place of the dried thyme at roughly three times the quantity — a teaspoon of dried is about a tablespoon of fresh, chopped.

Dietary Notes

  • For a vegetarian version, look for a plant-based Worcestershire sauce — the anchovies in the standard bottle are what makes ordinary worcestershire sauce unsuitable, not anything else in it.

Keeping What's Left

Keeps in the fridge for up to 4 days and thickens on standing, so loosen it with a splash of stock when reheating. Freeze for up to 3 months, though the potatoes break down a little more on thawing.
