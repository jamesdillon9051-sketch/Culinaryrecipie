The Case for Pan-Seared Pork Chops

Pork chops have a reputation for being dry, and the reason is that they are overcooked and under-seasoned. Modern pork is lean, and a chop cooked past 63°C turns tough and pale. Three habits fix it. A short brine of salt and sugar in water, 30 minutes for chops of this thickness, seasons the meat through and helps it hold on to its juice. The chop is cooked in a very hot pan, so that it browns before the centre overheats. And the thermometer decides when it is done, not the clock: 63°C in the thickest part, then a 5-minute rest, in which it climbs a few degrees more. A little pink in the middle at that temperature is normal and safe. Butter, garlic and thyme go in at the end and are spooned over the chops for the last minute or two.

Making Pan-Seared Pork Chops, Step by Step

Tap a step to highlight it, or turn on Cook Mode for large type and step-by-step focus.

  1. Stir the salt and sugar into the water until dissolved. Add the chops, making sure they are covered, and leave for 30 minutes.

  2. Lift the chops out and pat them very dry with paper towels. Mix the pepper, garlic powder and paprika and rub them over the chops.

  3. Heat a large heavy frying pan over medium-high heat until very hot. Add the oil, then lay in the chops without crowding them, cooking in two batches if the pan is small.

  4. Cook for 4 minutes without moving, until deep golden brown. Turn and cook the second side for 3 minutes. Stand the chops on their fat edge for 1 minute to render it.

  5. Add the butter, garlic and thyme. When the butter foams, tilt the pan and spoon it over the chops for 1 to 2 minutes, until the thickest part reads 63°C.

  6. Move the chops to a plate, rest them for 5 minutes and spoon over the pan juices.

Notes From the Method

Brine for no longer than 30 minutes. Chops left in salty water for hours turn spongy and taste cured.

Dry the chops thoroughly after brining. A wet surface steams, and the chop goes grey instead of golden.

Take them off the heat at 63°C. The chop climbs a few degrees while it rests, and every degree beyond that dries it out.

Swaps and Variations

  • An eighth of a teaspoon of garlic powder per clove is the stand-in usually given when you are out of fresh garlic; it will not brown the same way, and it burns easily, so add it with the liquid.

Leftovers

Best eaten at once. Keep leftovers in the fridge up to 3 days and reheat gently, covered, in a low oven or slice them thinly for sandwiches.
