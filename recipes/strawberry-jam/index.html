Strawberry Jam

Bright red strawberry jam made from 1 kg of ripe berries, sugar and lemon juice, boiled to setting point. Makes four jars, and is better than any in the shops.

Why This Is Worth Making

Strawberry jam is the first jam that most people ever make, and it is one of the best. The fruit is ripe and sweet, the method is simple, and the result is a bright red preserve that tastes of fresh strawberries far more than anything from a jar in the shops. Strawberries are low in pectin, the natural substance that makes a jam set, so the recipe relies on the pectin in the lemon juice and on cooking the jam to the right temperature, and it needs no added pectin. The berries are first mixed with the sugar and lemon juice and left for an hour, so the sugar pulls out the juice and the fruit holds its shape as it cooks. The mixture is then brought slowly to the boil to dissolve the sugar and boiled hard until it reaches the setting point, 105°C on a thermometer, or when a spoonful on a chilled saucer wrinkles when it is pushed with a finger. This is the crucial test, so do not skip it. Jam that is not boiled long enough is runny, and jam that is boiled for too long becomes dark and sticky and loses its fresh taste. The finished jam is potted into sterilised jars while it is hot and sealed at once. It keeps for a year in a cool, dark cupboard, and is good on toast, scones, in a sponge cake, stirred into yoghurt, or on rice pudding.

Making Strawberry Jam, Step by Step

Tap a step to highlight it, or turn on Cook Mode for large type and step-by-step focus.

  1. Put 2 small saucers in the freezer. Toss the strawberries with the sugar and lemon juice in a large bowl, cover and leave to macerate for 1 hour, until the sugar has drawn out the juice.

  2. Tip the fruit and juices into a wide, heavy pan and heat gently, stirring, until the sugar has completely dissolved.

  3. Bring to a full boil and boil hard for 15 to 20 minutes, stirring often and skimming off any foam, until the jam reaches 105°C / 221°F on a sugar thermometer.

  4. To test the set, spoon a little jam onto a chilled saucer, wait 1 minute and push it with a finger. If the surface wrinkles, it has set. If not, boil for 3 minutes more and test again.

  5. Take the pan off the heat and let it stand for 10 minutes, stirring gently, so that the fruit is evenly spread. Ladle into hot sterilised jars to within 5 mm of the top, seal at once and cool.

Notes From the Method

Choose ripe but firm strawberries. Overripe fruit is low in pectin and gives a soft, syrupy jam.

Use a wide pan, not a tall one. A wide pan lets the water evaporate quickly, so the jam reaches setting point before the fruit turns dull.

Test the set with a chilled saucer, and take the pan off the heat while you do it. Jam keeps boiling and can pass the point while you are checking.

Keeping What's Left

Unopened jars keep in a cool, dark cupboard for up to 1 year. Once opened, keep in the fridge for up to 4 weeks.
